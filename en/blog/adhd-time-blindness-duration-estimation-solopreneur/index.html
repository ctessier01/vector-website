ADHD and Time Blindness: Why "This'll Take 20 Minutes" Is Almost Always Wrong

8 min read

"Twenty minutes, tops." You start. You look up, the task isn't even done, and two hours have passed. Not twenty-five minutes, not thirty-five. Two hours.

It didn't happen once. It's your entire relationship with time. You announce durations with total confidence, and they turn out wrong almost every time, always in the same direction, too short.

If you recognize yourself here, chances are you're not simply "bad with time." You may be living what specialists call time blindness, a common trait in ADHD brains, and it explains far more than your lateness.

And it doesn't only affect diagnosed people. Many solopreneurs live a milder version of this fuzzy relationship with time, without ever putting a name to it. Whether you have a diagnosis or not, the mechanism is the same, and so are the fixes.

Time blindness, explained

For most people, time has a texture. They feel, without checking the clock, the difference between five minutes and half an hour. That internal clock runs in the background and warns them when time is slipping away.

We take that clock for granted, precisely because it's invisible when it works. We don't realize how much we lean on it to decide to leave, to switch tasks, to wrap up. It's only when it's missing that you see everything it was doing, silently, in the background.

For an ADHD brain, that clock is faulty, sometimes outright missing. Five minutes and fifty minutes have the same texture, which is to say none. Time isn't felt, it exists in only two states, "now" and "not now." That's time blindness, and it isn't a figure of speech, it's a real cognitive difficulty, documented by organizations like ADDA.

This missing sense of time has cascading consequences. You misjudge durations. You start too late because the deadline doesn't "weigh" yet. You dive into a task and surface three hours later, never having seen the time pass. These aren't separate flaws, they're the same mechanism in different forms.

One example comes up constantly among solopreneurs, the "just one last thing" before an appointment. You have fifteen minutes, you launch into a "quick" task, and you show up late to the call, surprised, again, that time slipped away. It's not that you don't care. It's that nothing, inside, flagged that the fifteen minutes were up.

Multiply that small failure across a whole day, and you get the picture, a string of tasks that each ran long, a schedule that never matched reality, and the nagging sense that you're somehow always behind. Not because you did less, but because the map you were working from was wrong from the start.

It's not a willpower flaw

You'll be told to "pay more attention," to "manage yourself better." That advice assumes you have a sense of time you could simply use more seriously. But you can't pay more attention to a signal you don't receive.

Duration estimation depends on executive functions, the group of mental processes that plan, sequence, and anticipate. In many ADHD brains, those functions work differently. It's not a matter of effort or character, it's a matter of wiring. The same person, capable of intense focus on what fascinates them, can be unable to judge how long a mundane task will take.

That's exactly why classic productivity systems often fail the ADHD brain, they assume a relationship with time you don't have. And even without a diagnosis, this tendency to underestimate durations affects nearly everyone, just more sharply when the internal clock is missing.

This distinction isn't just consolation. It concretely changes the strategy. If the problem were willpower, the fix would be to push harder, and you've surely tried that already, with no lasting result. If the problem is a missing signal, the fix is to replace that signal, not to punish yourself for not hearing it. That's a completely different approach, and it's the only one that holds over time.

You don't underestimate time out of carelessness. You misjudge it because, for you, it has no texture to judge.

Compensate without self-blame

You don't repair a faulty internal clock with willpower. But you can replace it with external markers. The general idea, stop relying on your felt sense of time, and make it visible and concrete outside yourself.

The simplest technique, and one of the most effective, comes down to a single number.

+50%
add half to every estimate, in other words multiply it by 1.5. A rule widely recommended for ADHD brains, because that factor matches real time far better than your first instinct

Twenty minutes becomes thirty. An hour becomes an hour and a half. It seems crude, but that's exactly the point, you stop negotiating with a sense of time that lies to you, and you apply a mechanical correction that is actually reliable. Other external markers help in the same spirit, visible timers, fixed anchors in the day, or time blocking, where each block makes a duration tangible on screen.

Another powerful habit, keep a record of your real durations. For one week, note how long a few recurring tasks actually take. You'll be surprised, and that little log becomes your new reference, far more reliable than your intuition. Next time, you no longer estimate blind, you remember.

Stack two or three of these markers, the +50% rule, a visible timer, a log of real durations, and you've rebuilt, piece by piece, a rough version of the clock you don't have inside. It won't be perfect. It doesn't need to be. It just needs to beat guessing.

Worth remembering

The +50% rule isn't pessimism. It's a prosthetic for a function you lack. You're not resigning yourself to being slow, you're correcting a measurement that was skewed at the source.
