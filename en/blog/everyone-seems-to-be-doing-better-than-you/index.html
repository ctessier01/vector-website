Everyone Seems to Be Doing Better Than You

5 min read

Comparing yourself to your peers doesn't just dent your mood in the moment. Repeated often enough, the habit retrains your attention toward what you lack instead of what you're building. And when you work alone, no coworkers around, no yardstick, that lens quietly becomes the only one you view your own work through.

The trouble isn't that other people are doing better than you. It's what you compare, and what you compare it to.

The 10 PM scroll that shrinks you

Because the scene keeps repeating, always the same. It's 10 PM. You scroll LinkedIn one last time. One person announces a launch. Another mentions a "record year." A third thanks their growing team. You close the app a little smaller than when you opened it, without quite knowing why.

That moment is not harmless. At night your guard is down. The day is over, you're tired, your judgment is duller. That's exactly when the feed of other people's wins lands hardest. Not in the morning, when you have momentum. At night, when you're taking stock.

And the tally is rigged in advance. You compare your whole day, hesitations and all, the file that wouldn't move, the email left unanswered, against one carefully chosen sentence from someone who also had a day full of hesitations. They just didn't post that part.

The result isn't a passing bad mood. A now-classic study, "Seeing Everyone Else's Highlight Reels," run by researchers at the University of Houston and published in the Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, found it isn't time on social media that weighs on your mood, but the social comparison it triggers. In other words, the scroll isn't what hurts. It's what the scroll pushes you to do: measure yourself against people who only show their best moments.

You're not comparing yourself to their life. You're comparing yourself to what they chose to show of it.

Backstage versus trailer

Name the sleight of hand precisely, because it's the thing that warps everything. You live your reality from the inside, continuously, unedited. You know your doubts, your real numbers, the contract that nearly fell through, the week you barely invoiced anything. Them, you only see a trailer. Two or three seconds cut from months of footage.

Those numbers are an image, not a statistic. But the ratio holds. Nobody posts the rejected quote, the afternoon spent producing nothing, the March cash gap. So you compare your full cut to their trailer, and you wonder why you lose every time.

Add the algorithm, which doesn't hand you a neutral sample. It pushes whatever gets reactions: the announcements, the big numbers, the milestones. Ordinary days, the ones that look like yours, simply don't exist in your feed. You're watching a world where nothing happens but peaks, and asking yourself why yours has flat stretches.

Worse: the more someone is struggling, the more they sometimes polish the facade. The shiny announcement hides as much as it reveals. You draw conclusions about other people's lives from the one slice they decided to let you see.

Why going solo amplifies the poison

In a job, there are natural correctives. A coworker who lets slip that the flagship project ran three months late. A lunch where someone admits the "record year" hid a divorce. An office is full of counter-evidence that deflates the trailers.

Alone, you have none of that. Your only window into other solopreneurs is precisely the one they've made up. You get the highlights with no making-of. Same soil as the solopreneur's loneliness: with no witnesses, every external signal takes on outsized weight, because nobody's there to put it back in its place.

On top of that sits a basic confusion about what "success" means. The feed sells you one definition: bigger, faster, a team, a "scale." You end up comparing yourself to a path you often don't even want. That's the whole point of growth at all costs: plenty of solopreneurs aren't trying to get big, they're trying to make a good living from their craft. Measuring yourself by other people's growth is failing an exam you never signed up for.

Measuring against your own path

The way out isn't repeating "don't compare yourself." Your brain compares, it's wired in, willpower won't unplug it. The way out is changing the reference point. Not other people. You, a year ago.

That comparison is the only honest one, because you have access to both ends. You know the you from twelve months back, with the worries you had then, the clients you had then, the skills you had then. And you can measure, concretely, the distance covered. The problem is that the distance is invisible until you've written it down somewhere. Memory erases past difficulties once they're behind you, which leaves you feeling like you always knew how to do what you do today.

You're comparing an inside you know by heart to a facade you've never walked into.

The concrete move isn't complicated. Keep a record of what you get done, week after week. Not to brag. To have, in front of you, tangible proof that you're moving, on the nights your brain tries to convince you otherwise. That record of your own wins is the best counterweight to the feed of everyone else's.

There's a second reason this works. Progress you can see is progress you can trust. Vague pride fades by morning. A concrete list of what changed since last year holds up against a bad night, because it's evidence, not a pep talk. You stop needing to win an argument with yourself, you just read the record and the argument dissolves.

Worth keeping

When comparison grabs you at night, close their feed and open yours. The only useful question isn't "am I doing better than them?" but "am I doing better than a year ago?"

Other people don't have a better life than you. They have a better-edited feed. The difference between feeling behind and feeling in your place doesn't come down to what they publish. It comes down to what you choose to look at, and how often you choose to look at your own work instead of theirs.
