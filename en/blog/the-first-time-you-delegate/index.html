The First Time You Delegate

5 min read

It's ten past midnight. You're resizing images for a page that ships tomorrow. Fourth time this week you've done a task someone else would do 80% as well as you.

You do it anyway. Same reason as last time: "by the time I explain it, I'll have finished it."

That sentence is true. Which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

The midnight task you keep "because it's faster"

Short term, the math holds. Explaining the task takes thirty minutes. Doing it takes twenty. On any single instance, you're right to keep it.

The trouble is you rerun that math every time, and every time it returns the same answer. Over twelve months you paid the twenty minutes fifty times instead of investing the thirty once.

It isn't stupid reasoning. It's local reasoning. It optimizes tonight and ignores the year. And since nobody shows up at the end to hand you the total, you never see the bill.

You're not alone in this. A survey of 251 U.S. entrepreneurs, commissioned by a virtual assistant firm and reported in Forbes, puts a number on the scale of it.

36%
of the average entrepreneur's work week goes to administrative tasks, while the work that actually moves the business waits

Thirty-six percent. More than a third of your week. The survey was commissioned by a company that sells delegation, so hold the exact figure loosely. The order of magnitude won't shock any solopreneur who has honestly looked at their own days.

The real brake isn't money

Ask those entrepreneurs why they don't delegate and the answers are more embarrassing than a budget problem.

27%
say they enjoy doing the easy tasks themselves
25%
figure it would be faster to just do it themselves
17%
have nobody they feel they could delegate to

Look hard at the first one. Twenty-seven percent enjoy it. And that makes complete sense. An easy task hands you an immediate small win. It ends. It checks off. The hard work, the work that counts, checks off nothing for a long time.

Resizing images is over in twenty minutes. Rethinking your offer never really ends. Guess which one your brain reaches for at midnight.

The second number, the "faster if I do it," is the rationalization. It's true, and it's the truest-sounding excuse you own, which is why it survives for years without ever being examined.

The third one, having nobody to hand it to, is the only real logistical obstacle in the list. And that's precisely the one that's changing fastest right now.

You don't keep these tasks because they're hard. You keep them because they're easy, and they make you feel like you're getting something done.

The 80% test

The first instinct when people talk about delegation is to hunt for what to give away. Wrong starting point. Start from the opposite end: what can nobody but you do?

The answer is almost always short. Your judgment on a client file. Your creative signature. The decisions that set the direction of the business. The relationships with people who trust you.

Everything else is a candidate. And the filter is simple: if someone else can do it 80% as well as you, it isn't yours anymore. Not 100%. Eighty.

Take a week and write down what you actually do, honestly, in thirty-minute chunks. Not to punish yourself. Just to see it. Most solopreneurs who run that exercise find two or three recurring blocks they've been holding onto for years without ever deciding to.

That's where it jams, of course. Perfectionism tells you 80% isn't enough. Same trap as in shipping instead of polishing. The last twenty percent often costs more than the first eighty, and nobody sees it but you.

The honest question isn't "will it be as good?" It's "what do I do with the hours this frees up?" If the answer is "more 80% tasks," then no, it doesn't pay. If the answer is "the work nobody else can do," the math flips instantly.

Delegating without hiring

The big fear underneath all this is the employee. A salary, obligations, management. It's a full identity change, and plenty of solopreneurs simply don't want it. That's a legitimate choice, and one we looked at in why not all solopreneurs want to scale.

But delegating isn't hiring. Between "I do everything" and "I have a team" sits an enormous, underused space.

A freelancer for one recurring task, five hours a month. A virtual assistant for inbox triage and scheduling. An accountant for the thing you never should have touched. And increasingly, software agents for the repetitive work that requires no judgment at all.

None of that requires a payroll, a contract of employment, or a conversation about benefits. It requires one task, one person or one tool, and a willingness to be slightly worse at something you were never paid for anyway.

Start small, on one task, the most boring and most repetitive one you have. Document it once, in ten minutes, while you're doing it. That documentation is the real investment. Not the money.

And accept in advance that the first round goes badly. The work comes back imperfect, you fix it, you tell yourself you were right all along. That's normal. The second round is better. The fifth is better than what you were doing, because someone doing that task fifty times a month gets better at it than you doing it four times.
