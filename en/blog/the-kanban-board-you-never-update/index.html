Why Your Kanban Board Lies About Your Progress

5 min read

Why does your Kanban board lie? Because it only moves when you move it.

A board shows your last update. If that update happened last Thursday, you're looking at last Thursday while believing you're looking at today.

It's a small mechanical detail, and it completely changes what you can conclude when you open the board in the morning.

The twelve cards stuck in "In Progress"

Friday, 4 p.m. Your board proudly shows twelve cards in the "In Progress" column. You go through them one by one. Seven finished days ago. Two belong to a project that died in June. Three are actually moving, and it took you four minutes to work that out.

You pay those four minutes every time you want to know where you stand. The board promised a bird's-eye view. It hands you an inventory to sort.

"In Progress" is always the column that rots first, for the same reason every time: moving a card to "Done" takes a gesture nothing forces you to make. The work itself is finished. You've moved on. The card stays where you left it.

There's a second, phantom column nobody creates: the work that exists but never got carded. Tuesday's email that created a new expectation. The request made on a call. The follow-up you promised a supplier. None of it shows on the board, which means that even well maintained, it displays a partial version of your week.

Your board doesn't measure your progress. It measures how consistently you maintain it, which is a completely different metric.

A board reflects your discipline before your progress

Kanban was designed on factory floors, then adopted by software teams. In both cases, several people look at the same board every day. Updating is an act of communication: if you don't move your card, your colleague doesn't know what to pick up.

Working alone, that spring disappears. Nobody is waiting on your card. You're both the one updating and the only one reading, which means you can skip the update with no immediate cost. So you skip it, because you're busy doing the work.

Notice the order of operations there. Updating the board is an accounting task about work already done. Under pressure, accounting always loses to production, and it should. The board doesn't fail because you're disorganized; it fails because it asks for attention at the exact moment attention is scarcest.

The board turns into an artifact of good intentions. It shows you the last time you had the energy to tidy it, not the shape of your week.

The gap always widens at the worst moment. In a quiet week you keep the board current, and it does little for you because you already know where you stand. In a heavy week you don't touch it, and that's exactly the week a reliable view would have mattered. The tool is least accurate precisely when you need it most.

It's the same trap we described when Notion becomes a second job: the tool ends up demanding upkeep you can't give it, and the upkeep costs more than the information it produces.

What a board does well, and what it will never do

Kanban isn't worth throwing out. It does some things very well, and it's fair to say so.

What a board does well
  • Show a client the state of a project in one image
  • Cap work in progress when you respect the limit
  • Surface the bottlenecks that keep landing in the same place
  • Act as shared memory when there are several of you
What it won't do for you
  • Update itself during a week when you're underwater
  • Tell you what to do today rather than tomorrow
  • Account for your real deadlines and your real workload
  • React when an urgent job lands Tuesday morning

The right-hand column describes exactly the work left on your plate. A board files information; it doesn't decide. And when you have three clients, two deadlines in the same week, and invoicing to do, deciding is precisely the hard part.

That gap between filing and deciding explains most abandoned tools. You adopt a system hoping it will tell you what to do Monday morning. It hands you a tidy list, and the original question stays open. After a few weeks the distance between what you expected and what you got is wide enough that you stop opening the app.

The cost of hand-maintaining the tool meant to help you

There's a direct cost, in minutes, to tidying a board every week. There's a bigger indirect cost that's harder to see.

Every tool you maintain by hand becomes one more place where information lives. Asana's Anatomy of Work Index put the average knowledge worker at around ten applications a day, switching between them roughly 25 times. For a solopreneur, that's a loop between email, the board, the calendar, and notes, with nobody synthesizing.

The result: the overview exists nowhere. You rebuild it in your head every morning from four partially current sources. That's the machinery we took apart in five tools, zero overview.

And when you eventually abandon the board, you tend to conclude you're bad with systems. The simpler explanation: you adopted a tool designed to be maintained by a team, and you're one person.

The math is worth doing honestly. Fifteen minutes of weekly tidying is thirteen hours a year. Not enormous on its own. The expensive part is the stretch where you stopped tidying without stopping trusting the board, because that's when you make decisions on stale information.

A system that depends on your discipline to stay true will start lying the day you're swamped, which is exactly the day you need it.
