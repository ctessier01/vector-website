Why Coming Back From Vacation Hurts More Than Leaving

5 min read

We always blame the vacation. "It went by too fast." "The return is brutal." As if the rest itself were the problem.

It isn't. The wall you hit on the first Monday has nothing to do with having left. It comes from the way everything lands on you at once, unordered, the day you reopen your laptop. Eighty-four emails, three "any update?", and no idea where to restart.

The vacation didn't create that chaos. It paused it. And a pause always restarts all at once.

The first Monday and the 84 emails

You open your laptop around 8:30. The coffee is good, you're rested, you even feel a little invincible. It lasts about forty seconds, the time it takes the inbox to finish loading.

Then everything arrives at once. The emails, yes, but mostly what's inside them: a client following up, a decision waiting, a file that moved without you, an invoice to send, two requests you'd forgotten before leaving. Your brain, running on nothing but sun twelve hours ago, suddenly has to hold thirty open threads at the same time.

And solo, nobody held the fort. No coworker to give you a ten-minute rundown, no assistant who sorted the urgent from the rest. Everything that piled up is waiting for you, whole, in one place, at the same second.

So you do what a brain does facing an avalanche: you flit. You knock out three easy emails for momentum, open a heavier file and close it right back, hop from one channel to the next. You're moving, but on the surface, on what's quick rather than what matters. And by noon, the rested feeling has already worn thin.

Why the return hurts more than the departure

The departure has something the return doesn't: a deadline. Your vacation date is coming, and it forces you to sort, to warn people, to close what's pressing. So you leave in a kind of organized descent, pushed by the countdown. The return has no deadline organizing it: everything you froze thaws at once, unordered, and nothing was sorted while you were gone.

There's a real chemical swing too. A meta-analysis on vacation effects, by de Bloom and colleagues, confirmed a clear pattern: rest genuinely improves wellbeing, mood, and energy, peaking around the eighth day. But those gains fade fast. Within one week back at work, wellbeing has already dropped to where it was before you left.

That's not a doom sentence, it's information. If the benefit melts in a week, how you organize that first week matters more than the length of the vacation itself. A well-run return protects your rest. A return you absorb blindly liquidates it in one morning.

Same mechanism as in the Thursday wall: overload doesn't fall on you by chance, it arrives because everything converges at the same moment with nothing spreading it out.

Prepping the return before you leave

The good news: the return is largely decided before you even leave. Future you, the one on the first Monday, depends entirely on the ground today's you leaves behind.

01
Close loops before you goEvery half-open file is a load that waits for you intact. Close what you can, even imperfectly. A finished file doesn't jump you on the way back.
02
Leave yourself an entry pointBefore you close the laptop, write a short note: "when you're back, start here." Three lines. It's the thread the pre-vacation you, who still has all the context, hands to the returning you, so it doesn't start from zero.
03
Push your official returnIf you're back Monday, tell clients you're back Wednesday. Those two buffer days aren't theft, they're the margin that absorbs the avalanche instead of dumping it onto your health.
04
Block your first day for re-entryNot production, not delivery. Sorting. A day whose only goal is regaining control, not moving a deliverable forward.

None of these steps needs heroic organization. They just need you to accept one simple idea: re-entry is a real phase of work, not a switch you flip back on.

Getting back to what matters without facing it all at once

The real trap of the first day is letting the loudest thing decide for you. You open the channel that blinks most, answer the most insistent person, and let the volume of the follow-ups set your priorities. By day's end you've handled what shouted loudest, not what mattered most.

The first day back isn't for producing. It's for sorting.

Sorting means splitting three piles. What's genuinely urgent, what only looks urgent, and what can wait for the week. Most of the "any update?" notes land in the second pile. Loud, not critical.

The reason this reorders your whole day is subtle. Left to its own devices, your attention answers the loudest and the newest, not the heaviest. A cancelled contract and a newsletter both sit there unread, and the noisy one usually wins. Deciding the order yourself, once, in the morning, is the single move that separates a first day that rebuilds your control from one that hands it to whoever shouted last. It costs fifteen minutes and it changes the entire shape of the week that follows.

Do
  • Reserve the first day for sorting, not delivery
  • Reopen one channel at a time
  • Warn two or three clients of a slight delay, before they chase you
Avoid
  • Letting the loudest thing set your priorities
  • Reopening email, messages, and calls all at once
  • Going full speed at 9 AM on the first Monday

That reflex of sorting before producing echoes the summer slowdown: the hinge periods of the year aren't run like normal weeks, they need their own playbook.
