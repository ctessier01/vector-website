Why Other People Are Filling Your Calendar

5 min read

Why is your calendar full of things you didn't choose? Because an empty calendar is an open invitation, and other people read it before you do.

Look at your week. Five "quick" calls, two "got fifteen minutes?", a follow-up that's been sliding, a check-in from last week. Somewhere in there was supposed to be the project that actually moves your business forward. It isn't there.

And you didn't agree to anything unreasonable. Taken one at a time, every request made sense.

The week you did nothing but respond

Friday arrives and you feel like you worked hard. You did. You answered everyone, you held your calls, you cleared irritants. And yet the list of things that actually matter hasn't moved an inch since Monday.

This kind of week has a recognizable signature. Everything that moved came from an outside request. Nothing that moved came from you.

When you work solo, that slide is easy to make. A client writing is revenue. A prospect who wants to chat is opportunity. Turning down a slot feels like turning down money, so you open the slot. Multiply by four clients and you spend the week in receiving position.

The difference from permanent emergency mode, which we covered in an earlier piece, is subtle but real. Here, nothing is on fire. There's just a calendar filling up in the order other people arrive.

A full calendar mostly measures one thing: who got to your time first.

An empty calendar is an open invitation

The mechanism is simple. Free time carries no label: nobody knows you meant to move your site rebuild forward in that block. Booked time defends itself. The scheduling link you politely dropped in your signature can't tell the two apart.

Large organizations live an amplified version of the same thing. In its special report on the infinite workday, Microsoft analyzed aggregated Microsoft 365 usage signals and reported three findings worth your attention.

Half of all meetings fall between 9 and 11 a.m. or 1 and 3 p.m., right in the concentration peak
57% of meetings happen with no calendar invite sent ahead of time
One interruption every two minutes on average across eight hours, among the most-pinged users

Two caveats before going further. Those numbers describe employees inside large companies, not independents. And the last two cover the top fifth of users by volume, not the average of everyone. Your reality isn't theirs: no manager summons you, and nobody adds you to a sixty-five-person meeting. The mechanism, though, is the same. Requests aim at the hours when you're most mentally available, because those are the hours when everyone is.

Put plainly: your two best blocks of the day are exactly the ones other people book first.

There's a shift in habits underneath this too. The scheduling link made booking free for whoever asks: three clicks, no negotiation, no social cost. Getting thirty minutes of your time used to require an email, a proposal, a counter-proposal. That friction filtered out a share of the requests. It's gone, and nothing replaced it.

The real price of a slot you didn't choose

Take an ordinary Tuesday. Five thirty-minute calls, spread between 9 a.m. and 4 p.m. In calendar terms that's two and a half hours out of eight. Five and a half hours left, which on paper is plenty.

Except those five and a half hours don't exist in one piece. They exist as twenty-five, thirty-five, and forty-minute fragments, minus the prep before each call and the settling-down after. Deep work, the kind that needs ninety uninterrupted minutes to produce anything, has nowhere to land.

That example is illustrative, not a measured average. But look at last Tuesday through that lens and you'll probably recognize the shape. It's also why context switching costs you more than the time you can see going by.

So "got fifteen minutes?" bills in two parts: the fifteen minutes, plus the block it snaps in half.

Then add what the call takes on either side. Five minutes to find the file, reread the last exchange, remember where things stood. Then, once you hang up, one or two new actions that just came into existence and need to land somewhere before you forget them. A thirty-minute call rarely occupies thirty minutes of your day.

And that arithmetic shows up nowhere. Your calendar faithfully displays the thirty minutes. It never displays the fifteen around them, or the deep-work block that never happened.

Book it before you get booked

The fix is mechanical. If your real work has no slot, it gets no room. The only moment you can give it one is before the requests arrive.

What protects your time
  • Block your deep-work slots early in the week, before opening messages
  • Narrow your scheduling link to a few specific windows
  • Cluster calls into the same half-days
  • Answer "how about Thursday at 2?" instead of "whenever works"
What opens it to others
  • Letting your scheduling link expose the whole week
  • Accepting the default slot out of reflexive politeness
  • Handling each request on its own, without looking at the full week
  • Keeping your deep-work blocks in your head instead of your calendar

None of this requires becoming difficult. Offering two windows instead of your entire calendar is still a courteous answer, and it's usually faster for the person on the other end. The approach overlaps with time blocking, with one difference: the goal here fits in a single verb, claim, and it has to happen before someone else does.

One objection comes up constantly: "my clients will think I'm less available." In most cases, what a client remembers is the quality of what you deliver and how reliably you come back to them. Someone who answers "Thursday at 2" with confidence reads as serious as someone who leaves everything open, often more so. Permanent availability rarely sells on its own.

Worth keeping

Before accepting a slot, ask one question: what moves somewhere else if I say yes? If you don't know the answer, that's your signal that your calendar no longer reflects your real workload.

Your calendar remains the only inventory you truly own, and the supply is strictly fixed. Every slot handed to someone else is a slot taken from somewhere, even when you can't see where.
