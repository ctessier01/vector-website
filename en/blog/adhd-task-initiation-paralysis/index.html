ADHD: When Starting Is Harder Than Doing

6 min read

The task takes twenty minutes. You've been circling it for two hours: a coffee, an open tab, a notification, another coffee. You know exactly what to do. You just can't start.

Around three in the afternoon, you finally sit down. Eighteen minutes later it's done, properly, without any real effort. Then you think back to the two hours before, and you still don't understand what happened.

This piece is about one specific mechanism: task initiation. It's written first for solo operators living with ADHD, diagnosed or strongly suspected. If that's not you, the mechanism below still applies, in a milder form.

The wall in front of the task

The work itself isn't the issue. Once you're moving, you're often good at it, sometimes very good, and the dreaded task gets handled in far less time than you feared.

The block lives in the space just before: the moment you have to move from "I should" to "I'm doing it now." Cognitive psychology has a name for that moment. It's called task initiation, and it's a full executive function, like working memory or inhibition. It has its own cost and its own failure modes.

From the outside it looks like plain procrastination. From the inside it's something else: you want to do the task, you intend to do it, you can feel the deadline approaching, and nothing starts. The intent is intact. The entry point refuses to open.

When you work alone, that wall charges you twice. Once in hours. Once in self-judgment, because nobody else sees those two hours, and you're the only candidate available to take the blame.

The real cost of a twenty-minute task usually lands in the three seconds before it.

What happens when the start won't fire

Since Russell Barkley's work in the 1990s, ADHD has been understood mainly as a self-regulation disorder rather than a simple attention deficit. The core issue sits in the ability to start, hold back, and steer behaviour. That distinction changes what kinds of solutions actually help.

Starting an action requires something like a go signal, and that signal depends partly on anticipated reward. One of the reference models, Sonuga-Barke's dual pathway, describes two mechanisms working together: reduced sensitivity to distant rewards, and a strong aversion to delay. Translated into your day, the rule is simple. The vaguer, flatter, or further-off-paying a task is, the more expensive its entry.

That's why a reply to a client email can sit for three days while a full rebuild of your offer takes off on its own on a Tuesday night. How hard something is to start tracks clarity, novelty, and how close the reward feels, far more than it tracks volume of work.

Worth knowing: this profile is far from rare among founders. A BDC survey of 1,463 Canadian entrepreneurs found that 7% had a professional ADHD diagnosis, and that nearly one in four were affected once you include those reporting symptoms without a diagnosis.

Why "just push through" doesn't work

The standard advice assumes the fuel is missing. You must be short on willpower, on discipline, on reasons to get up. So the fix is to add more: a stricter morning routine, a louder reminder, a penalty if you fail.

It works once. With enough adrenaline, you clear the wall by force. The next time, the same method costs more, and the effect wears off fast. What you force once doesn't turn into a capability.

Worse, every failed attempt adds an emotional layer to the task. The document you didn't open yesterday becomes the document you haven't opened in four days, and that weight stacks on top of the original starting cost. The task didn't grow. Its entry did. That's the same loop covered in our piece on ADHD and solopreneurship: conventional organizing systems demand exactly the function that's struggling.

A task you dread costs more to open than to finish.

So the way out runs through a lower entry cost, rather than through an extra dose of motivation.

Deciding the first micro-action in advance

There's one technique with solid evidence behind it, and it targets exactly this point. It's called an implementation intention: instead of stating a goal, you write an if-then plan that specifies when, where, and how you'll begin.

Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran pooled 94 independent tests covering more than 8,000 participants to measure the effect. The finding: if-then plans produce a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, and getting started is among the effects the meta-analysis documents.

The reason is mechanical. When the trigger situation arrives, the decision has already been made. You no longer have to pick a starting point at the exact moment picking costs you the most.

01
Write the task as an action verb"Tremblay proposal" doesn't tell you what to do. "Draft the first paragraph of the Tremblay proposal" does.
02
Shrink the entry to something almost sillyOpen the document and write one sentence. The goal is to get through the door. The rest sorts itself out once you're inside.
03
Tie it to a concrete trigger"When I hang up my ten o'clock call, I open the Tremblay document." A real moment in your day, one you can spot without checking a clock.

Three honest caveats. This technique wasn't designed for ADHD, and its average effect comes mostly from general-population studies. It has to be written down, not just thought about. And it replaces neither medical follow-up nor professional support if you need either.

External structure that starts for you

Deciding in advance works. Deciding in advance fourteen times a week, for fourteen tasks across five clients, turns back into a part-time planning job. And that job demands exactly the functions that cost you the most.

It's also why strategies that move the decision outside your head keep showing up in the approaches recommended for ADHD. Body doubling runs on that principle: another person's presence lowers the entry cost without adding anything to your discipline. An external deadline does the same. So does a plan that's already built.

In the morning, "where do I start?" already has an answer, and you can change it if it doesn't fit. None of that treats ADHD. It simply removes one decision from the pile, at the exact spot where that decision costs the most.

Worth keeping

No ADHD? The mechanism still holds, just lighter. Any task that's vague, dull, or paid off far in the future costs more to start than to do. Deciding the first action in advance lowers that cost for everyone.

Next time you've been circling a task for an hour, stop hunting for the missing willpower. Look at the entry instead: can you name, in one sentence, the very first physical move? Most of the time the answer is no, and that's where the block actually sits.

Write that move down. Attach it to a moment in your day. Then forget the rest of the task until you're inside it.
