Rented or Owned: Why the Newsletter Beats the Algorithm

5 min read

On beehiiv, a platform hosting tens of thousands of newsletters, the average open rate cleared 41% in 2025. On the Facebook side, Sprout Social treats roughly 5.5% of followers as a good organic reach rate, and its industry benchmarks often sit between 1 and 3%.

Those two numbers don't measure the same thing, and we'll get into that. They still point at something that holds: access to a list you own is predictable, access to an audience you rent is not.

The 12,000-view post, then silence

You publish something good on a Tuesday. Twelve thousand views, forty comments, a handful of DMs. You figure you've finally cracked it.

Thursday you publish something just as good. Two hundred views. You changed nothing about your method, your topic, your format, or your posting time. Something changed somewhere else, inside a system you have no access to.

That's the nature of a rented channel. It works, until the day it works less, and you get no notice, no explanation, and no appeal. The metrics you stare at aren't yours either, which feeds the exact feeling described in this piece on constant comparison.

And if you look at what those twelve thousand views actually produced, the picture gets clearer still. One contract, at best. Sometimes nothing at all. Sometimes a good conversation with someone who will never buy. Reach is platform currency. It doesn't convert to clients on its own, and you can't bank it.

A channel you don't control can hand you reach. It can't promise you'll see it again tomorrow.

Renting or owning: where your audience actually lives

The distinction comes down to one very simple technical question: can you reach these people without going through an intermediary that decides for you?

If your audience lives on LinkedIn, Instagram, or TikTok, the answer is no. The platform owns the list, chooses who sees what, and can change its rules overnight without telling you. You hold a right of use, not a right of ownership. It's a lease, and the landlord can revise it any time.

If your audience lives in an email list you can export whenever you want, the answer is yes. You can switch providers, migrate the file, and write to whoever you want, whenever you want. The relationship stays between you and the person who said yes.

That difference turns genuinely strategic in 2026, for a reason we unpacked in our piece on the end of free traffic: search engines increasingly answer directly, and the traffic they used to hand websites for free keeps shrinking. When two of your three attention taps belong to someone else, the third one stops being optional.

One honest caveat: email isn't a dependency-free channel either. Your sending provider can close your account, and deliverability rides on spam systems you don't control. The difference is the file. If your provider disappears tomorrow, you export your list and import it somewhere else the same day. Nobody can do that with their Instagram followers.

A rented audience makes you visible. An owned audience makes you reachable.

Three caveats apply, and they matter.

First, the two metrics don't measure the same thing. Reach counts eyeballs; an open counts an email opened. Those are two different definitions of reaching someone.

Second, the 41% comes from a single platform reporting on its own publishers. It isn't the average of the email ecosystem, and newsletters hosted there are probably better made than average.

Third, since Apple started shielding activity in Mail, a share of reported opens fire automatically, with nobody reading. Published open rates since 2021 run inflated, more so the larger your share of Apple Mail subscribers.

What survives all of that: the person on your list took a deliberate action, gave you an address, and that address stays with you. The person who tapped "follow" promised nothing, and will never belong to you.

Social to get found, email to stay

None of this means abandoning social platforms. They do one thing email does not do at all: they get you discovered by people who weren't looking for you. That work is valuable, and no newsletter replaces it.

The problem starts when discovery is your only channel. You restart from zero every week, you pay the algorithm in content, and you accumulate nothing. Email plays the other role: it keeps you present with people who already found you.

So the natural sequence looks like this. Social brings attention. Your site or article holds it briefly. Your newsletter keeps it. And for a lot of independents who dislike selling, it's also the most comfortable channel, because it lets you stay useful without having to perform, the way we described in this piece on selling.

That leaves the content question. The good news is you don't have to produce anything new. An article you already published, a detailed answer you gave a client by email, an observation you've made three times this week: all of it turns into a send. The people on your list didn't ask you to become a media company. They asked you to keep talking to them.

Start small, this week

The most common reason for not having a list is the belief that you'd need a real editorial plan, a calendar, a template, a lead magnet, and software. That's false, and it costs a lot of people several years.

The first email can be four paragraphs sent to eleven people who already know you. It will feel too small to matter. That feeling is the whole obstacle, and it never goes away on its own, it just gets outgrown.

What keeps a list alive
  • A cadence you can actually hold, even if it's monthly
  • A clear promise at signup: what you write about, how often
  • Writing to specific people rather than to an audience
  • A signup form on the pages people genuinely read
What kills it
  • Waiting until you have enough subscribers for it to "be worth it"
  • Importing contacts who never asked to hear from you
  • Three sends in January, then nothing until September
  • Turning every send into a pitch

One practical legal note: Canada's anti-spam law governs consent, sender identification, and a working unsubscribe mechanism, and other jurisdictions have their own rules. A form where the person signs up themselves points in that direction; a scraped contact list does not. For what actually applies to your situation, it's worth checking with a professional.

Worth keeping

Forty subscribers who open your email beat four thousand people who tapped "follow" two years ago. The first number is an audience. The second is a statistic.

Which platform wins in 2027? Nobody knows, and that's exactly the point.

The useful question is plainer: if your main account vanished tomorrow morning, how many people could you still reach on your own? If the answer is zero, you know what to build this week.
